How to Spike a Volleyball: Approach, Timing and Shot Choice

By the Spike Rally team · Updated 6 November 2026 · 8 min read

The spike is the most exciting skill in volleyball and the one that scores most points. Hitting hard is only part of it. Good attackers win with footwork, timing and choosing the right spot, and those three can be learned by anyone.

1. The approach

The approach is the run-up that turns forward speed into a high jump. Most players use a four-step approach. For a right-handed hitter the steps are right, left, right-left; left-handers mirror it.

  1. First step (right): a small, slow step in the direction of the set. It is mainly for timing and aim.
  2. Second step (left): a slightly longer step as you speed up.
  3. Third step (right): the longest and fastest step. Swing both arms back behind you as you take it.
  4. Closing step (left): bring the left foot down next to the right, a little in front. Plant both heels and roll onto the toes as you bend your knees.

The last two steps should be quick, almost a "step-close" rhythm. Many coaches teach beginners a three-step approach (left, right-left for right-handers) first, then add the opening step once the timing is solid.

2. The jump

Swing both arms forward and up hard as you take off; the arm swing adds a surprising amount of height. Jump up, not forward. A hitter who drifts into the net risks a net fault and loses power. Try to take off far enough behind the ball that it will be slightly in front of your hitting shoulder at the top of your jump.

3. The arm swing

At the top of the jump, draw your hitting arm back with the elbow high, at least at shoulder height, like pulling back a bow and arrow. Your non-hitting arm points up at the ball to help aim and balance. Then:

  • Rotate the hips and shoulders towards the target.
  • Swing the arm through fast, reaching high. Contact the ball at the highest point you can, slightly in front of your body.
  • Hit with an open hand, palm on the top-back of the ball. Snap the wrist so your fingers wrap over it. This puts topspin on the ball and makes it dip into the court.
  • Follow through down past your hip on the same side, and land on both feet with your knees bent.

4. Timing the set

Most mistakes come from starting the approach too early or too late. A useful rule for a normal high outside set is to start your approach when the ball is at the top of its flight from the setter. For faster sets you start earlier, sometimes before the setter has even touched the ball. Watch the ball leave the setter’s hands, then adjust your first step towards where it is going. If you find yourself waiting under the ball, you started too early; if you are reaching behind you, you were late.

5. Choosing a shot

A hard spike into a well-formed block is usually a point for the other team. Good attackers look at the block and the defence and pick a shot that avoids both.

ShotHowWhen to use it
Cross courtHit diagonally across the court.The longest distance in the court, so the safest hard swing. Good when the block is on the line.
LineHit straight down the sideline.When the outside blocker is late or sets up too far inside.
Cut shotA sharp angle close to the net, made by turning the wrist.When the defence sits deep and the block leaves the angle open.
TipPush the ball softly over or around the block with the finger pads.When defenders are all deep. Use it sparingly or it becomes predictable.
Roll shotA slower swing with heavy topspin that drops behind the block.Against a big block when you are off balance or the set is tight.
Off the blockAim at the outside hand of the blocker so the ball flies out.When the set is tight to the net and the block is strong. Also called "using" or "tooling" the block.

6. Reading the defence

Experienced hitters glance at the other side during the approach. Where is the block forming? Is there a gap between the two blockers? Is anyone covering the short middle of the court? Even beginners can learn to notice one thing: which side of the court is empty. Hitting to open floor is worth more than hitting hard.

Drills to improve your spike

  • Approach without a ball: do ten approaches in a row, focusing only on the step-close and the arm swing. Film yourself if you can.
  • Wall spikes: stand a few metres from a wall and hit the ball down into the floor so it bounces up off the wall, then hit it again. This trains wrist snap and topspin.
  • Self-toss hits: toss the ball high in front of you, approach and hit it over a net. Start with a lower net if needed.
  • Target hitting: place cones or towels in the corners and middle of the far court and call your target before each attack.

Common mistakes

  • Drifting forward instead of jumping up, which leads to net touches and weak hits.
  • Dropping the elbow, which sends the ball into the net or long.
  • Hitting with a flat palm and no wrist snap, so the ball floats out.
  • Always hitting the same shot. A good block learns your favourite angle in one set.
