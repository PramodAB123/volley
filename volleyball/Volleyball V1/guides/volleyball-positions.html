Volleyball Positions and What Each Player Does

By the Spike Rally team · Updated 6 November 2026 · 7 min read

The rules number players by zone, but teams think in positions: a role each player keeps all match while they rotate through the zones. Knowing the six roles makes a match far easier to follow, and it tells you which job suits you if you are joining a team.

Zones are not positions

A player’s zone (1 to 6) is where they must stand when the ball is served, and it changes every time the team rotates. A player’s position is their specialist job. As soon as the serve is hit, players move from their zone to the spot their position needs. You will see the setter sprint to the right of the net and the hitters spread out to the left, middle and right before the ball has even crossed the net.

Most teams use six roles: one setter, two outside hitters, two middle blockers and one opposite, with a libero coming in for the middles when they are in the back row.

Setter

The setter is the playmaker, often compared to a quarterback or a point guard. They take the second touch on almost every rally and decide who attacks. A good setter delivers the ball to the same spot every time, at a height and speed the hitter likes, and hides their choice for as long as possible so the blockers cannot read it.

  • Where: on serve receive and in defence the setter moves to just right of centre at the net, about a metre from it.
  • Key skills: clean hand setting, footwork to get under bad passes, decision-making, and the occasional surprise "dump" over the net on the second touch.
  • Good fit if: you like reading the game and making other people look good.

Outside hitter (left side)

Outside hitters attack from the left front, the most common place a set goes, especially when the pass is poor and the setter needs a safe option. They also pass in serve receive and play defence in the back row, so they are usually the most complete players on the team.

  • Where: left front when in the front row, left or middle back in the back row.
  • Key skills: attacking high sets against a double block, passing, back-row defence.
  • Good fit if: you want to touch the ball a lot and be trusted in tight moments.

Opposite (right side)

The opposite plays across from the setter in the rotation, so when the setter is in the back row the opposite is in the front row and vice versa. At high levels the opposite is often the main scorer: they hit from the right side, attack from the back row, and block the other team’s outside hitters. Because they usually do not pass serves, they can save their energy for attacking.

  • Where: right front, right back.
  • Key skills: big attacking, blocking, and setting when the setter has to take the first ball.
  • Good fit if: you are a strong hitter, and left-handed players are especially valuable here.

Middle blocker

Middle blockers are usually the tallest players. In the front row they block across the whole net, moving left and right to join the outside blockers, and they hit fast, low sets called quicks right in front of the setter. A quick attack forces the other team’s middle to jump with it, which opens space for the outside and opposite hitters.

  • Where: middle front. When they rotate to the back row the libero usually replaces them.
  • Key skills: reading the setter, lateral speed along the net, a fast arm swing, timing with the setter.
  • Good fit if: you are tall, quick on your feet and like a battle at the net.

Libero

The libero is the defensive and passing specialist, recognisable by a different coloured shirt. They play only in the back row and can come on and off for back-row players without counting as a substitution. Liberos take a big share of serve receive, dig hard-driven attacks and often take the second touch when the setter digs the first. The rules stop them from serving, blocking or attacking above the net in international play.

  • Where: left back or middle back, depending on the team’s system.
  • Key skills: passing accuracy, reading attackers, reaction speed, communication.
  • Good fit if: you love defence and do not need to score to enjoy a point.

Defensive specialist

A defensive specialist (DS) is like a libero without the special rules. They wear the normal shirt, come in as a regular substitution, often for a hitter who is weak in the back row, and they may serve. Many teams bring on a DS who also has a tough serve when a front-row player rotates to serve.

How positions fit together: 5-1, 6-2 and 4-2

A team’s system describes how many hitters and setters it uses.

  • 5-1: one setter who sets in all six rotations, and five hitters. This is the most common system at a good level because the setter’s consistency is so valuable. In half the rotations the setter is in the front row, which leaves only two front-row hitters but gives the setter the option of a dump.
  • 6-2: two setters, each setting only while in the back row and attacking as a hitter in the front row. There are always three front-row hitters, but the team has to keep two setters in rhythm.
  • 4-2: two setters who set from the front row. It is simple to run and common with beginners, but there are only two attackers at a time.

Which position should you play?

If you are new, try several. Passing well will make you useful in any role, and most coaches move players around until they find a fit. Height helps at middle and opposite, but there are excellent short setters, outside hitters and liberos at every level. Ball control, footwork and communication matter in every position.
