Volleyball Rules Explained: A Beginner’s Guide

By the Spike Rally team · Updated 6 November 2026 · 8 min read

Volleyball looks chaotic the first time you watch it: six players a side, a ball that rarely touches the floor, and a referee whistling things you did not see. The rules behind it are simple, though. This guide covers the indoor 6v6 game as it is played in clubs, schools and international events, in the order you need them.

The goal of the game

Two teams of six stand on either side of a net. Each team tries to ground the ball on the other team’s court, or force the other team into a mistake, while keeping the ball off its own floor. A team may touch the ball up to three times before it has to go back over the net. Every rally ends with a point for one side, so the game moves quickly and there is never a rally that "does not count".

The court and the net

An indoor court is 18 metres long and 9 metres wide, split into two square halves of 9 by 9 metres by the centre line under the net. Three metres from the net on each side is the attack line, which separates the front zone from the back zone. The lines are part of the court, so a ball that touches any part of a line is in.

The net is 2.43 metres high for men and 2.24 metres for women at senior level, lower for younger age groups. Two flexible antennae stick up from the net above each sideline. The ball has to cross the net between them; a ball that hits an antenna, or passes outside it, is out.

Scoring: rally point

Since 1999 the international game has used rally point scoring: whoever wins the rally wins the point, no matter who served. The team that wins the point also serves next.

  • A set is won by the first team to reach 25 points with a lead of at least two. If the score is 24–24, play continues until one side is two clear (26–24, 27–25 and so on).
  • Most matches are best of five sets. The first team to win three sets wins the match.
  • If a match goes to a fifth set, that deciding set is played to 15 points, still won by two, and the teams change ends when the leader reaches eight.

Recreational leagues often shorten this to best of three, or to timed games, but the win-by-two rule almost always stays.

Three touches and how you may hit the ball

Each team gets a maximum of three contacts to return the ball. The classic pattern is pass, set, attack: the first player controls the ball, the second places it for a hitter, and the third sends it over hard. Teams are free to send it over earlier, and a sneaky second-touch attack by the setter is a real weapon.

  • The same player may not touch the ball twice in a row. The exception is a block: touching the ball at the net while blocking does not count as one of the three touches, and the blocker may play it again.
  • The ball must be hit, not caught or thrown. Holding it, or guiding it in a scooping motion, is called a lift or catch fault.
  • Any part of the body may be used, including feet.
  • On the first contact of a team, the referee is more lenient: a pass that touches two parts of the body in one action is allowed. On a set, two separate contacts are a double.

Serving

Every rally begins with a serve from behind the end line. The server has eight seconds after the referee’s whistle to hit the ball, and may serve from anywhere along the width of the end line. Stepping on or over the end line before contact is a foot fault. A serve that clips the top of the net and drops over is legal and stays in play.

The serving team may not hide the server or the flight of the ball from the receivers by standing close together in front of them. That is called a screen and is a fault.

Rotation and positions on the court

The six players are numbered by zone. Seen from behind your own team, zone 1 is right back, 2 is right front, 3 is middle front, 4 is left front, 5 is left back and 6 is middle back. The player in zone 1 serves.

When your team wins a point on the other team’s serve, you side out: you win the serve and all six players rotate one place clockwise. The player in zone 2 moves to zone 1 and becomes the new server. Over a set, every player passes through every zone, which is why volleyball players have to be able to do a bit of everything.

At the moment of the serve each player has to be in the right order relative to the players next to them. This is the overlap rule, and it is explained in detail in our rotations guide. Once the ball is served, players may move anywhere on their own side.

Front row and back row

The three players in zones 2, 3 and 4 are the front row. Only they may block, and only they may attack the ball from above the height of the net while standing in front of the attack line. A back-row player may still attack, but has to take off from behind the attack line. Landing in front of the line afterwards is fine; jumping from in front of it is not. This rule is why you will see big hitters launch spikes from well behind the 3 metre line in top-level matches.

Common faults

FaultWhat it means
Ball outThe ball lands outside the lines, hits the antenna, the ceiling or an object outside the court.
Four hitsA team touches the ball four times before sending it over.
Double contactOne player touches the ball twice in a row (outside the first-touch and block exceptions).
Net touchA player touches the net between the antennae while playing the ball.
Centre lineA player’s whole foot crosses the centre line into the other court. Part of the foot on or over the line is allowed.
Reaching overPlaying the ball on the other team’s side before their attack. Blockers may reach over only after the other team has attacked.
Rotation faultPlayers are out of position when the ball is served, or the wrong player serves.
Back-row attackA back-row player attacks a ball that is completely above the net from in front of the attack line.

The libero

Each team may name a libero, a defensive specialist who wears a different coloured shirt. The libero may replace any back-row player without using up a substitution, and usually comes in for the middle blockers when they rotate to the back. Under international rules the libero may not serve, block or attack a ball from above the height of the net, and if the libero sets the ball with the fingers from in front of the attack line, a teammate may not attack that ball above the net. In return, teams get an expert passer on the floor for most of the match.

Substitutions and time-outs

In international play each team has six substitutions per set and two 30-second time-outs per set. A player who starts the set and is substituted out may come back once, but only into the same spot in the rotation. School and club rules are often more generous, so check your league’s version.

Your first game: what actually matters

If you are about to play for the first time, you do not need to memorise every rule. Remember these five and you will be fine:

  1. Three touches maximum, and never two in a row by the same person.
  2. Do not touch the net.
  3. Rotate clockwise when your team wins the serve back.
  4. Serve from behind the line, and stay behind it until you hit the ball.
  5. Call the ball loudly ("Mine!") before you play it. Most beginner points are lost to two people watching the ball land between them.
