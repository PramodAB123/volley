Volleyball Rotations Explained (With Diagrams)

By the Spike Rally team · Updated 6 November 2026 · 7 min read

Rotation is the rule that confuses new players most. It sounds complicated, but it comes down to two ideas: a fixed serving order that moves one place every time you win the serve back, and a short list of who has to stand in front of or beside whom when the ball is served.

The six zones

Each half of the court is split into six zones. Looking from behind your own team towards the net:

  • Front row, left to right: zone 4, zone 3, zone 2.
  • Back row, left to right: zone 5, zone 6, zone 1.

The numbers run anticlockwise starting from the server, which makes the rotation itself clockwise.

When you rotate

You rotate only when your team wins a rally that the other team served. This is called a side out. You do not rotate when you win a point on your own serve; the same player simply serves again.

When you rotate, every player moves one zone clockwise: 2 goes to 1, 1 goes to 6, 6 goes to 5, 5 goes to 4, 4 goes to 3 and 3 goes to 2. The player who has just arrived in zone 1 serves. If you are ever unsure, remember that the player who was right front is the next server.

The overlap rule

At the instant the ball is served, the receiving team and the serving team must both be in their correct rotational order. It is not about standing exactly in your zone. It is about your position relative to your neighbours:

  • Front to back: each front-row player must be closer to the net than the back-row player directly behind them. Zone 4 in front of 5, zone 3 in front of 6, zone 2 in front of 1.
  • Side to side: in each row, the left player must be further left than the middle, and the middle further left than the right. Zone 4 left of 3, 3 left of 2; zone 5 left of 6, 6 left of 1.

What counts is the position of the players’ feet on the floor. A player only has to be slightly in front of, or slightly to the side of, the right neighbour. That small amount of freedom is what lets teams hide their setter and put their best passers where the serve is likely to go.

The server is the exception. Once they are behind the end line they are not part of the overlap check at all.

After the serve: switching to real positions

As soon as the server hits the ball, everybody is free to move. Teams use this to put each player in their specialist spot. A typical front row switches so the outside hitter goes left, the middle goes to the middle and the opposite goes right, whatever zones they started in. In the back row the setter, if they are back there, runs to the net, and the libero and outside hitter settle into left and middle back.

This is called switching. It is why a rotation can look strange at the moment of serve, with players crowded in odd places, and then look perfectly normal a second later.

Serve receive formations

When receiving, teams arrange themselves so that the best passers cover most of the court and the setter is as close as possible to the net without breaking the overlap rule. Common patterns are:

  • Three-person receive: two outside hitters and the libero share the court. The most common choice at a good level.
  • Four-person receive: a "W" shape without the central player. Helpful against strong servers or with less experienced passers.
  • Five-person receive: everyone except the setter in a "W". Common with beginners because it leaves fewer gaps.

Thinking in "rotation one" to "rotation six"

Coaches name rotations by where the setter is when the team is receiving. "Rotation one" means the setter starts in zone 1, "rotation six" means zone 6, and so on. In a 5-1 system there are three rotations where the setter is in the back row and three hitters are in front, and three where the setter is in the front row with only two hitters. Teams usually know which of their six rotations score most easily and which are the weakest, and they try to start sets in their best one.

Common rotation mistakes

  • Rotating anticlockwise. Say it out loud the first few times: right front becomes server.
  • Rotating after winning a point on your own serve. You only rotate after a side out.
  • Moving before the serve. Switching early gives the other team a free point for an overlap.
  • The wrong person serving. Many teams have the next server shout their name so everyone checks.
