Volleyball Blocking and Defense: How to Stop a Spike

By the Spike Rally team · Updated 6 November 2026 · 8 min read

Points are won at the net, but matches are often won by defence. A team that blocks well and digs a few hard spikes every set takes the other team’s best weapon away and gets extra chances to score. Blocking and digging work together, so it helps to understand both.

What a block is for

A block has three jobs. The best result is a stuff, a block that sends the ball straight down on the attacker’s side for a point. The second is a soft block or touch that slows the ball so your defenders can dig it. The third is to take away part of the court: even if the attacker hits around the block, they can only hit where your defenders are waiting. A block that does none of these is wasted, so blockers and defenders plan together.

Blocking stance and hands

  • Stand an arm’s length from the net, feet shoulder-width apart, knees slightly bent and hands up at shoulder height, palms facing the net.
  • Jump straight up. Drifting sideways in the air pushes the ball out or into the net.
  • Reach over the net, not just up, and press your hands towards the middle of the other team’s court. Keep your fingers spread and your thumbs pointing up.
  • The outside blocker turns the outside hand slightly inward so balls deflect back into the court rather than out.
  • Keep your eyes open and watch the hitter’s arm, not the ball.

Footwork along the net

Blockers move along the net with either a shuffle (side steps, for short distances) or a crossover (a running step across the body, faster for longer distances). Middle blockers often need a two- or three-step crossover to reach the pin in time, finishing with both feet square to the net before they jump.

Timing the block

Against a high outside set, blockers usually jump slightly after the hitter, because the hitter is reaching up from further away. Against a quick set in the middle, they jump almost together with the hitter. The best clue is the hitter’s arm swing: jump as the hitter’s arm starts forward.

Read blocking and commit blocking

There are two main blocking strategies.

  • Read blocking: blockers wait to see where the setter sends the ball, then move and jump. It is slower, so the block is sometimes late or only one person, but it is rarely fooled.
  • Commit blocking: a blocker decides before the set to jump with a specific hitter, usually the quick attacker. If the guess is right, the block is fast and strong. If it is wrong, the real attacker faces fewer blockers or none at all.

Most teams read by default and commit at certain moments, for example against a team that always sets the middle on a perfect pass, or against one great hitter in the last points of a set.

Base defence and the perimeter

While the other team is passing and setting, defenders stand in their base positions, ready to move. Once the set goes out, they move to their defensive spots around the block. A common system is the perimeter defence: the two back-corner defenders play deep near the sidelines, the middle-back stays deep in the centre, and the off-blocker (the front-row player not blocking) drops back to cover sharp angles and tips. Defenders should stand outside the shadow of the block, where the ball can actually come.

How to dig a hard spike

  1. Get low and still. Feet wider than shoulders, weight on the balls of the feet, hands apart in front of the knees. Be stopped when the hitter contacts the ball.
  2. Read the hitter. The shoulder and the arm tell you the direction of the attack.
  3. Platform early. Bring the forearms together with a flat platform and angle it so the ball rebounds up towards the middle of your own court.
  4. Absorb, do not swing. A hard spike already has enough speed. Keep your arms quiet and let the ball rebound; give a little with the body if needed.
  5. Aim high to the middle. A dig that goes three or four metres up in the middle of the court gives the setter time to arrive.

Balls you cannot reach with a platform can be dug with one arm, a "J-stroke" with the hand, or a sprawl. The key habit is to keep playing every ball until it hits the floor.

Communication

Blockers and defenders talk throughout the rally: the middle calls the block ("Go with the outside!"), defenders call "Tip!" when they see a soft shot, and whoever is going for a dig shouts "Mine!". Good defence is impossible in silence.
